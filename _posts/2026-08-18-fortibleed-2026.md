## Background

FortiBleed was disclosed in June 2026 after researcher Volodymyr Diachenko
exposed an attacker's open directory containing their full operational
environment: GPU cracking infrastructure, credential datasets, post-exploitation
tooling, and a working SSL VPN config into a victim network.

The campaign targeted internet-facing FortiGate appliances. Attackers exported
device configuration files, cracked the legacy SHA-256 password hashes offline
using a Hashtopolis-managed GPU cluster, then used recovered credentials to
pivot into victim Active Directory environments. From there: LDAP enumeration,
Kerberoasting, SMB share access, and staged exfiltration over SSH.

The attack IP in this lab `193.8.187.42` is a confirmed campaign IOC
documented by Recorded Future, CloudSEK, Arctic Wolf, and OSec as the
credential-validation and exfiltration staging node.

## Scenario

Your organisation's threat hunting team flagged anomalous outbound activity from
an internal Windows server after business hours. A packet capture was pulled
from the affected network segment. You have been tasked with reconstructing
the attacker's movements from the network evidence alone.

## Walkthrough

### 1. Identifying the attacker

Opening the capture in Wireshark and applying a broad filter (`ldap or kerberos or smb2`)
immediately reveals the attack: all malicious traffic originates from a single source.

```
ldap or kerberos or smb2
```

The address `193.8.187.42` sources every meaningful exchange in the capture. A DNS query for
`dc01.cascadelogistics.local` precedes the attack chain: the attacker resolving the
domain controller before enumerating it.

### 2. LDAP enumeration

Filtering on `ldap` and expanding the `searchRequest` packet reveals the filter
the attacker used:

```
ldap
```

The filter field reads `(servicePrincipalName=*)` which is a standard Kerberoasting
reconnaissance query. It asks the domain controller to return every account
with a registered service principal name, along with the `sAMAccountName`.
Three accounts come back: `DC01$`, `krbtgt`, and `svc_sql`. Only `svc_sql` is
a user account with an SPN: the obvious target.

![](/screenshots/1forti.png)

### 3. Kerberoasting

Filtering on `kerberos` and locating the TGS-REQ (message type 12) shows the
attacker requesting a service ticket for `MSSQLSvc/db01.cascadelogistics.local:1433`: the SPN registered to `svc_sql`.

```
kerberos
```

The ticket is issued with etype 23 (RC4), meaning it can be cracked offline
with a standard wordlist. The attacker walks away with a hash they can
attack without further access to the network.

### 4. Share access and collection

SMB2 traffic reveals the next phase. Filtering on `smb2` shows a tree connect
to `\\172.16.14.10\IT-Admin$` followed by `Create` and `Read` requests for two files:

```
smb2
```
![](/screenshots/2forti.png)

- `firewall_backup_notes.txt` contains VPN credentials and FortiGate management
  details, including a still-active backup admin account
- `passwords.txt` a plaintext credential list left on a shared drive

The file contents are readable in the SMB stream directly in Wireshark via
`Follow / TCP Stream` on the relevant connection. The stolen data includes
references to `fortiadmin`, `FortiGate`, and `vpn.cascadelogistics.com`: exactly
the kind of intel that enables the broader FortiBleed campaign to escalate
a perimeter breach into full internal access.

### 5. Exfiltration and threat intelligence

The final phase is a transfer of the stolen files to an external host. The
destination address `193.8.187.42` is the only non-RFC1918 IP in the
entire capture, making it unambiguous.

Searching `193.8.187.42` on VirusTotal.com returns community tags and relations
linking it to the **FortiBleed** campaign. Multiple vendor reports (Recorded
Future, CloudSEK, Arctic Wolf, OSec) confirm this IP as the credential-validation
and exfiltration staging node: distinct from the Hashtopolis GPU cracking
infrastructure at `85.11.187.8` but part of the same operation.

## Lessons learned

1. A single LDAP `searchRequest` with `(servicePrincipalName=*)` is enough to
   identify every Kerberoastable account in the domain. One packet, full target list.

2. RC4 (etype 23) service tickets are crackable offline with rockyou and no
   further network access. Enforcing AES-only (`msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes`)
   closes this without disabling Kerberos.

3. Plaintext credential files on SMB shares are a force multiplier for any
   attacker who gets past the perimeter. The `passwords.txt` and `firewall_backup_notes.txt`
   files in this lab turned a service account compromise into full VPN access.

4. A confirmed threat intel hit on the exfil IP (`193.8.187.42`) ties
   an otherwise isolated incident to a documented nation-scale campaign.
   OSINT pivoting on IOCs is not an afterthought: it is how you know
   whether you are dealing with a targeted attack or commodity infrastructure.
